Education Loan for Study Abroad: Banks vs NBFCs vs Global Lenders (2027)

How much you can borrow with and without collateral, realistic interest rates, the moratorium trap, tax benefits, TCS rules, documents, and a week-by-week plan to get a sanction letter before your visa.

The short version

  • Three kinds of lenders: public/private banks (lowest rates, usually need collateral above ₹7.5 lakh), NBFCs like HDFC Credila, Avanse, Auxilo and InCred (larger unsecured loans, higher rates), and global lenders like Prodigy Finance and MPOWER (no Indian co-applicant, loans in USD).
  • Typical 2026 rates: roughly 8.5–11% for secured bank loans, 10.5–14% for NBFC unsecured loans; global lenders charge variable USD rates. Your university, course, co-applicant income and collateral move the rate the most.
  • Interest builds up during the moratorium (course + 6–12 months). On a ₹40 lakh loan at 10.5%, about ₹8.4 lakh can accrue before your first EMI — paying it while you study saves lakhs.
  • Example: ₹40 lakh at 10.5% for 10 years ≈ ₹54,000 EMI; ≈ ₹65,000 if unpaid moratorium interest is added to the loan.
  • Money rules: loan-funded education remittances have no TCS; interest is deductible under section 80E for 8 years — but only in the old tax regime.

Secured vs unsecured loans

Secured (with collateral)

  • Backed by property, fixed deposits or LIC policies.
  • Lower interest, larger amounts, longer tenure.
  • Property valuation and legal checks add 2–4 weeks.
  • Best for large US/Canada/Australia budgets if your family owns suitable property.

Unsecured (no collateral)

  • Based on your admit, course, university reputation and the co-applicant’s income.
  • Higher interest; amount depends heavily on the university list the lender uses.
  • Faster — sometimes a sanction in 1–2 weeks.
  • Common for top universities and STEM courses.

Under the Indian Banks’ Association model scheme followed by many banks, loans up to ₹4 lakh need no margin or security, ₹4–7.5 lakh typically need a third-party guarantee, and loans above ₹7.5 lakh need tangible collateral. Many public-sector banks still follow this, which is why students with big budgets and no property go to NBFCs or global lenders.

Apply to three, not one

Get quotes from one public bank, one NBFC and (if eligible) one global lender in the same week. Use the best offer to negotiate — lenders do match rates and waive processing fees for strong profiles.

What decides your interest rate

  1. University and course: lenders keep tiered lists. A STEM master’s at a well-ranked university gets better terms.
  2. Collateral: property or FDs lower the rate the most.
  3. Co-applicant: a parent or sibling with stable, documented income and a good CIBIL score (750+).
  4. Your profile: academic record, test scores, prior work experience.
  5. Rate type: floating rates are linked to a benchmark (like the repo rate) and move over time; RBI rules mean banks can’t charge foreclosure penalties on floating-rate loans to individuals.
  6. Concessions: some banks give 0.5% off for women students or for paying interest during the course.

The moratorium trap (and how to avoid it)

Most education loans give a moratorium — no EMI during the course plus 6–12 months. But interest still accrues (usually simple interest) on whatever has been disbursed. If unpaid, it’s added to the principal when EMIs start.

Scenario: ₹40 lakh at 10.5%, 2-year course + 6 months, disbursed in two halvesResult
Interest accrued before first EMI≈ ₹8.4 lakh
EMI if you pay nothing during study (₹48.4 lakh over 10 years)≈ ₹65,300/month
EMI if you pay the interest while studying (₹40 lakh over 10 years)≈ ₹54,000/month
Total interest — unpaid moratorium vs paid≈ ₹38.4 lakh vs ≈ ₹33.2 lakh

Even paying part of the interest during the course — from family support or a part-time job — makes a big difference. Ask the lender whether paying interest during the moratorium lowers your rate.

Tax: section 80E and TCS

Interest deduction (section 80E)

  • The full interest (no upper limit) on an education loan from a bank or approved institution can be deducted from taxable income for up to 8 years from the year you start repaying.
  • It’s available only to the person who took the loan (student or parent as borrower) for the education of self, spouse, children or a student you are the legal guardian of.
  • It is not available in the new tax regime. If you repay from an Indian salary with large interest, compare both regimes each year.
  • The new Income-tax Act, 2025 renumbered sections from 1 April 2026 — the benefit continues, but your ITR form may show a different section number.

TCS on money sent abroad

  • Education payments abroad funded by a loan from a specified financial institution: no TCS (from 1 April 2025).
  • Self-funded education remittances: no TCS up to ₹10 lakh a financial year; 5% TCS on the amount above ₹10 lakh.
  • TCS isn’t a final cost — it’s credited against the payer’s income tax and can be claimed back in the ITR.

Timeline: from admit to disbursement

  1. Before admits (2–4 months ahead): check your co-applicant’s CIBIL score, gather income documents, get a property valuation if you’ll use collateral.
  2. Week 1 after your admit: apply to 2–3 lenders; ask for written sanction terms.
  3. Weeks 2–4: receive the sanction letter. Many visas accept it as proof of funds — the UK, Canada, Australia and the US consulates look at loan sanctions alongside bank balances (see each country guide for specifics).
  4. Before the visa: for Germany, part of the loan funds the blocked account; for the UK, funds must sit for 28 days unless a sanction letter is accepted.
  5. Before the semester: tuition is disbursed directly to the university; living costs go to your forex card or account in stages.

Mistakes that cost students lakhs

  1. Borrowing for an expensive degree in a field with weak hiring for international graduates.
  2. Ignoring moratorium interest and being shocked by the first EMI.
  3. Accepting the first offer without comparing rates and processing fees.
  4. Choosing a long tenure for a lower EMI — and paying far more interest overall.
  5. Not reading clauses on rate resets, prepayment and what happens if you change university.
  6. Paying agents “loan processing” fees for something lenders do directly.
  7. Forgetting that if you return to India, an Indian salary must cover a loan priced for a foreign one.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get an education loan for abroad without collateral?

Yes. NBFCs such as HDFC Credila, Avanse, Auxilo and InCred, and some private banks, give unsecured loans for admits to universities on their approved lists, based on the course and the co-applicant’s income. Global lenders like Prodigy Finance and MPOWER lend without collateral or an Indian co-applicant for eligible programmes. Rates are higher than secured bank loans.

What is the interest rate on study abroad loans in 2026?

Indicative ranges are about 8.5–11% for secured loans from banks and about 10.5–14% for unsecured NBFC loans. Global lenders charge variable rates in US dollars. Your university, course, collateral and co-applicant profile decide the final rate.

Is TCS charged on education loan remittances?

No. From 1 April 2025, remittances for education financed by a loan from a specified financial institution attract no TCS. Self-funded education remittances above ₹10 lakh in a financial year attract 5% TCS on the excess, which can be claimed back in the income tax return.

Can I claim section 80E in the new tax regime?

No. The education-loan interest deduction is available only under the old tax regime. It covers the full interest for up to eight years from the start of repayment.

Does a loan sanction letter work as proof of funds for a visa?

In many countries, yes — the UK, Canada, Australia and the US generally accept education loan sanction letters from recognised lenders as part of the financial evidence. Germany needs the money in a blocked account, which the loan can fund. Check each country’s official visa page.

Should I pay interest during the moratorium?

If you can, yes. Unpaid interest is added to the principal when repayment starts, increasing both the EMI and total interest. On a ₹40 lakh loan at 10.5%, paying the moratorium interest can lower the EMI by about ₹11,000 a month.
